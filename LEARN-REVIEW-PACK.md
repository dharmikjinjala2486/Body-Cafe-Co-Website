# Learn — specialist review pack

22 October 2026 · Body Café Co. editorial · version 0.1.

**These drafts are not published on the site.** Each product guide's "What the research says" section shows only a status line ("A reviewed research summary is being prepared") plus links to the independent primary sources. The drafts below are for a qualified reviewer, for example a registered dietitian, pharmacist or physician, and for regulatory or claims review against FSSAI rules. They only go live once that review is done and the reviewer's name, credentials and review date are supplied.

Rules the drafts follow:
- They summarise what each source says, including its stated limitations.
- They describe ingredient research, never our finished products.
- They use no outcome, benefit or suitability claims of our own, no doses and no evidence grades beyond those the source itself gives.

## Iron (Iron Bisglycinate)

**Source:** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, *Iron: Fact Sheet for Health Professionals* (updated 4 Sep 2025). https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Iron-HealthProfessional/

- Iron is an essential component of hemoglobin, the red blood cell protein that carries oxygen.
- Supplement forms include ferrous and ferric salts and "iron amino-acid chelates". The sheet doesn't name iron bisglycinate specifically.
- Elemental iron percentages vary by compound: ferrous fumarate 33%, sulfate 20%, gluconate 12%.
- **Safety:**
  - Accidental overdose of iron-containing products is a leading cause of fatal poisoning in children under 6.
  - Intakes of 45 mg/day or more may cause gastrointestinal effects such as nausea and constipation.
  - The adult Tolerable Upper Intake Level is 45 mg.
- **Reviewer to decide:**
  - Which of these may be stated publicly, and how it fits FSSAI health-supplement claim rules.
  - Whether the US Upper Intake Level should appear, or the ICMR-NIN equivalent instead.

## L-Theanine

**Sources:**
- Vuong QV, Bowyer MC, Roach PD. *J Sci Food Agric.* 2011;91(11):1931–1939. This one is used publicly, for identity only.
- Williams JL, et al. "The Effects of Green Tea Amino Acid L-Theanine Consumption on the Ability to Manage Stress and Anxiety Levels: a Systematic Review." *Plant Foods Hum Nutr.* 2020;75:12–23. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11130-019-00771-5

Draft summary of Williams et al. (2020):
- The review included 9 randomised controlled trials.
- The authors suggest L-theanine "may assist in the reduction of stress and anxiety" in people exposed to stressful conditions, at the amounts studied.
- They state that larger and longer studies are needed, and that the evidence does not yet clinically justify using L-theanine as a therapeutic agent for stress and anxiety.

**Reviewer to decide:**
- Whether any of this can be published under FSSAI rules, or whether it must stay out entirely.
- Amounts studied must not be presented as a recommended dose.

## Vitamin D3 + K2 (with omega-3 as triglycerides)

**Sources:**
- NIH ODS, *Vitamin D: Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/
- NIH ODS, *Vitamin K: Fact Sheet for Health Professionals* (updated 29 Mar 2021). https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-HealthProfessional/

**Vitamin D:**
- It promotes calcium absorption in the gut and enables normal bone mineralisation.
- Excess amounts are toxic. The adult Upper Limit (19–50 years) is 100 mcg (4,000 IU).
- Trials suggest vitamin D with calcium may modestly increase bone density in some older adults, but "it is not clear whether they reduce falls and fracture rates."

**Vitamin K:**
- It is needed for proteins involved in blood clotting and bone metabolism.
- On bone, evidence is mixed: it is "unclear whether supplementation with any form of vitamin K reduces the risk of osteoporosis."
- Its cardiovascular role is unclear and still an active area of research.
- People taking warfarin and similar anticoagulants need consistent vitamin K intake.

**Already published:** the warfarin consistency note, sourced, in the guide's caution box.

**Reviewer to confirm:**
- The wording of that caution.
- Whether combined-formula research may be mentioned at all, given the omega-3 component.

## Omega-3 Fish Oil

**Source:** NIH ODS, *Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Omega3FattyAcids-HealthProfessional/

- **Heart health research:**
  - Seafood as part of a balanced diet is associated with heart health.
  - Fish oil and long-chain omega-3 supplements lower triglycerides.
  - Several later clinical trials found little or no benefit for major cardiovascular events.
  - Observational studies can't separate omega-3s from other seafood components or healthier habits.
- **Safety:**
  - Usually mild side effects, such as unpleasant taste, bad breath, heartburn and nausea.
  - Fish oil might prolong clotting times. People on anticoagulants should be monitored.
- **Reviewer to decide:** whether an anticoagulant caution belongs in the guide before the approved label is available.

## Myo & D-Chiro Inositol

**Sources:**
- Kiani AK, et al. *Eur Rev Med Pharmacol Sci.* 2021;25(5):2390–2402. This one is used publicly, for identity only.
- Showell MG, et al. "Inositol for subfertile women with polycystic ovary syndrome." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev.* 2018;12:CD012378. https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD012378_inositol-women-polycystic-ovary-syndrome

Draft summary of Showell et al. (2018):
- The review covered 13 randomised controlled trials with 1,472 participants.
- The authors were "uncertain whether MI improves live birth rate". Evidence was of low to very low certainty, with poor reporting, few trials and inadequate reporting of key outcomes.

**Reviewer to decide:** this is a condition-specific clinical topic. It's very likely unsuitable for publication on a health-supplement site under FSSAI rules, and it's recorded here so the reviewer has the evidence context.

## Creatine Chews

**Source:** NIH ODS, *Dietary Supplements for Exercise and Athletic Performance* (creatine section). https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/ExerciseAndAthleticPerformance-HealthProfessional/

- Creatine is made in the body (about 1 g a day, from glycine, arginine and methionine) and stored mainly in skeletal and cardiac muscle.
- It enhances performance during repeated short bursts of high-intensity, intermittent activity.
- Individual responses vary, and it appears to offer little value for endurance sports.
- It often leads to weight gain from water retention. Nausea, diarrhoea, muscle cramps and heat intolerance have been reported anecdotally.
- **Reviewer to decide:** whether exercise-performance research can be described for a chew whose formulation is still pending.

## Psyllium Husk

**Source:** European Medicines Agency, *Plantaginis ovatae seminis tegumentum* — herbal summary and monograph (Rev. 1, published 23 Jul 2013; review consultation 2024). https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/herbal/plantaginis-ovatae-seminis-tegumentum

- The EU monograph is for a herbal *medicinal* product, on the basis of well-established use.
- Its listed uses relate to constipation, stool softening, and raising dietary fibre intake.
- Its administration and safety notes:
  - Take with plenty of liquid; taking it without enough liquid can cause obstruction.
  - Keep it 30–60 minutes apart from other medicines.
  - Don't take it immediately before bed.
  - Avoid it with swallowing difficulties.
  - Allergic reactions can occur.
- **Reviewer to decide:**
  - Medicinal-product indications must not be transferred to a health supplement.
  - The public guide currently says only to follow the approved label's preparation directions, citing the EMA page for identity.
  - Confirm whether a "take with sufficient water" caution can be shown before the label is approved.
